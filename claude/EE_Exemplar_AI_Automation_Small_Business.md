## 1. Introduction

In 2025, 17.0% of small enterprises in the European Union used at least one artificial intelligence (AI) technology. Among large enterprises the figure was 55.0% (Eurostat, 2025). In the United States, a survey of firms between 2023 and 2024 found that over a quarter of AI users were using it partly to take over tasks that workers used to do, yet almost 95% reported no change in their headcount (Bonney et al., 2024). These two facts sit uneasily together. AI is spreading into small businesses, often to automate repetitive work, but the expected wave of job losses has not clearly arrived.

The question matters because small businesses have the least room for error. A small firm cannot easily absorb a failed software investment, and it cannot retrain staff the way a large company can. Most of the public debate about AI and work is based on large firms or on whole economies, so the effects on small businesses are often assumed rather than shown. This essay therefore asks: **To what extent do AI systems that automate repetitive tasks affect employment and productivity in small businesses?** Employment and productivity are treated together on purpose, because the core issue is the trade-off between them: whether doing the same work faster means needing fewer people.

The essay examines **artificial intelligence (3.6)**, including the algorithms that drive it, through the concepts of **change (2.1)** and **power (2.4)** in an **economic context (4.2)**, asking what the impacts and implications are for small-business owners and their employees. Change is the right lens because automation alters which tasks people do rather than simply whether jobs exist. Power matters because the evidence suggests the benefits of AI are not shared equally between small and large firms. The economic context was chosen over a social one because the strongest available evidence is economic: measured output, hours and employment.

The essay is based on secondary research only. Sources were selected if they were peer-reviewed articles, working papers from established research bodies (NBER, OECD), or official statistics (Eurostat, the US Census Bureau). News reports and consultancy marketing were excluded, because they rarely show how their figures were produced. The key claims are triangulated across three different types of evidence: controlled experiments on specific tasks; firm-level panel data that follow companies over time; and large representative surveys of businesses. Each type has a different weakness, so agreement between them is stronger than any one alone. A primary survey of local small businesses was considered and rejected. A small convenience sample could not separate the effect of AI from everything else changing in a business, so it would have added little reliable evidence.

The essay argues that AI automation of repetitive tasks raises productivity strongly at the level of the task, but that so far its direct effect on employment in small businesses is small. The more important impact is indirect: because larger firms adopt AI faster and gain more from it, the main employment risk for small businesses comes from competition, not from replacement. Section 2 explains how these systems work in small firms. Sections 3 to 5 examine productivity, employment and the size gap in turn, before Section 6 weighs the counter-arguments and Section 7 reaches a judgement.

## 2. The System in Context: How AI Takes Over Repetitive Work in Small Firms

### 2.1 From rules to learning: RPA, machine learning and generative AI

"AI automation of repetitive tasks" covers three kinds of digital system that work in different ways. The first is **robotic process automation (RPA)**: software "bots" that copy the clicks and keystrokes a person makes, such as moving invoice data from an email into an accounting system. RPA follows fixed rules written by a person, so it only works when a task is the same every time (Syed et al., 2020). In an early case study at the telecom company O2, RPA made routine back-office processes faster and more accurate (Lacity & Willcocks, 2016).

The second kind is **machine learning**. Instead of following rules, an algorithm is trained on past data to find patterns, for example to sort customer emails or predict stock levels. Its input is data, its processing is a statistical model, and its output is a prediction or decision. The third kind is **generative AI**: large language models (LLMs) that produce text, answers or code from a written prompt. LLMs widen automation from tasks that follow rules to tasks that involve language, such as writing replies or summarising documents. One estimate suggests a large majority of US workers have at least some of their tasks exposed to LLMs (Eloundou et al., 2024). Exposure, however, measures what is technically possible, not what firms actually do.

All three systems share one mechanism. They take over **routine tasks**: tasks that can be described as clear steps. Autor, Levy and Murnane (2003) showed that computers replace routine work and complement non-routine work such as problem-solving and dealing with people. This **task-based** view is the key to the research question, because it means automation acts on tasks, not on whole jobs.

### 2.2 Small businesses as a distinct context

Small businesses are not simply smaller versions of large firms. In this essay a small business means a firm with fewer than 50 employees, following the Eurostat size class. Where only data on small and medium-sized enterprises (SMEs, under 250 employees) exist, this is stated. Two features matter. First, one employee often does many different tasks, so automating one task rarely removes a whole job. Second, small firms have fewer resources. A systematic review of 71 studies found that the barriers SMEs report most often are lack of knowledge, cost, weak IT infrastructure and lack of usable data (Oldemeyer et al., 2025). Machine learning in particular depends on **datafication**: turning business activity into data that a model can learn from. A small shop with a few years of sales records has far less data than a national chain.

The essay focuses on software-based AI in offices and services. Physical robots are excluded as a topic, but firm-level studies of robot adoption are used where they are the best available evidence on how automation changes employment inside firms.

## 3. Productivity Gains Are Real but Bound to the Task

### 3.1 Measured gains on routine work

The strongest evidence in this essay is that AI makes routine tasks faster. Four studies using different methods agree on this. In a field study of 5,172 customer-support agents, a generative AI assistant raised the number of issues resolved per hour by 15% on average (Brynjolfsson et al., 2025). In a randomised experiment with 453 professionals doing writing tasks such as emails and short reports, ChatGPT cut the time taken by 40% and raised quality by 18% (Noy & Zhang, 2023). A trial with programmers found that an AI coding tool made them 55.8% faster, although three of its four authors worked for GitHub or Microsoft, the tool's maker and its owner, and the paper is not peer-reviewed, so it is the weakest of the four (Peng et al., 2023). Finally, in a field experiment with 758 consultants, GPT-4 users finished tasks 25.1% faster and completed 12.2% more of them (Dell'Acqua et al., 2026).

These tasks are exactly the kind of repetitive office work a small business does every day: answering customers, writing standard messages and producing simple documents. So the direction of the effect is clear. The limitation is where the evidence comes from. Most of it is from one large firm or from short, isolated tasks, not from a ten-person business doing the work in real conditions.

### 3.2 Who gains most: the levelling effect

The gains are not equal. In the customer-support study, less experienced agents improved by about 30%, while the most experienced barely improved at all (Brynjolfsson et al., 2025). Noy and Zhang (2023) also found that AI narrowed the gap between weaker and stronger writers. The mechanism is that the system learns from the best workers' past answers and passes that knowledge to beginners. For a small business, this could matter more than raw speed, because small firms often rely on staff who are new or have no specialist training.

However, the one field experiment that studied small-business owners directly gives a warning. When 640 Kenyan entrepreneurs were given a GPT-4 business assistant, there was no average effect on revenue or profit. Owners who were already doing well gained about 15%, but weaker performers did slightly worse (Otis et al., 2023). That tool gave advice rather than automating tasks, so the comparison is not exact. Still, it suggests the levelling effect inside a task does not automatically become a levelling effect between businesses. It depends on how well the owner uses the output.

### 3.3 Why task gains do not become firm gains straight away

Faster tasks do not automatically mean a more productive business. Brynjolfsson, Rock and Syverson (2021) describe a **productivity J-curve**: firms first have to spend on training, new processes and data before a new technology pays off, so measured productivity can fall before it rises. Survey evidence fits this. In Denmark, workers using AI chatbots saved only 2.8% of their working hours on average (Humlum & Vestergaard, 2025), far less than the experiments suggest. At the level of the whole economy, Acemoglu (2025) estimates AI will raise total productivity by no more than about 0.66% over ten years.

Firm data show the same pattern, and add a size gap. AI users across eleven countries are more productive than non-users, but the gap is largest in the biggest firms (Calvino & Fontanelli, 2023). Neither this study nor US Census data showing 11.4% higher labour productivity among adopters can prove that AI caused the difference, because more productive firms may simply adopt first (Acemoglu, Anderson, et al., 2022). The finding for small businesses is therefore conditional. AI clearly speeds up their repetitive tasks, but turning saved minutes into higher output needs reorganisation that small firms are least able to fund. This raises the next question: if tasks get faster, what happens to the people who did them?

## 4. Employment: Reallocation Inside the Firm, Not Mass Replacement

### 4.1 Displacement and reinstatement

The fear that AI destroys jobs assumes that a faster task means fewer workers. Acemoglu and Restrepo (2019) show why this is only half the picture. Automation has a **displacement effect**, as machines take over tasks people did, but new technology also creates new tasks, a **reinstatement effect**, which raises demand for labour. Whether employment falls depends on which effect is stronger.

How large the displacement effect could be depends heavily on method. Frey and Osborne (2017) estimated that 47% of US jobs were at high risk of automation, because they treated whole occupations as automatable. When Arntz et al. (2016) instead looked at the different tasks that individual workers actually do, the figure fell to about 9% across OECD countries. The task-based estimate fits small businesses better, because, as Section 2 showed, small-firm employees usually do a mix of tasks. Automating the invoice entry of an office assistant who also deals with customers changes the job but does not remove it.

### 4.2 What adopting firms actually do with labour

Firm-level studies, which follow the same companies before and after adoption, support this view, at least for the firms that adopt. In Spain, manufacturers that adopted robots increased employment by about 10% within four years, while their output grew by 20 to 25% (Koch et al., 2021). In France, adopting firms increased their hours worked by 10.9% (Acemoglu et al., 2020). In Canada, employment also rose after adoption, but the number of managers fell, and firms said they automated to improve quality rather than to cut labour costs (Dixon et al., 2021). For AI specifically, US firms that invested more in AI grew employment faster, by about 18% for a one-standard-deviation rise in investment (Babina et al., 2024).

Two findings qualify this positive picture, and both matter for small businesses. First, in the AI study the employment gain appeared only in the middle and largest thirds of firms. For the smallest third it was roughly zero (Babina et al., 2024). Second, Dutch data covering all private firms show that existing workers at firms that automate are more likely to leave and lose, over five years, about 9% of one year's earnings. These losses were larger in smaller firms (Bessen et al., 2025). So even when total employment in a firm holds steady, individual workers can pay the cost, and they pay more in small firms, which have fewer other roles to move them into.

### 4.3 Small labour-market effects so far

For generative AI, the effects on employment so far are small. In Denmark, chatbot adoption had no measurable effect on workers' earnings or hours, and the authors could rule out effects larger than 1% (Humlum & Vestergaard, 2025). In the United States, establishments exposed to AI reduced hiring for non-AI roles, but no effect was detectable at the level of whole occupations (Acemoglu, Autor, et al., 2022). Surveys of businesses point the same way. Among US firms using AI, 94.6% reported no change in employment, 2.8% an increase and 2.6% a decrease (Bonney et al., 2024). Among SMEs using generative AI in seven OECD countries, 83% reported no effect on staff needs, 9% a decrease and 6% an increase (OECD, 2025).

The detail in the OECD survey shows where the effect actually lands. About a third of users said AI reduced the workload of staff or the owner, and 14% said it reduced their use of outside contractors (OECD, 2025). This suggests the employment effect in small businesses is mostly invisible in headcount figures. It appears as hiring that does not happen, contractors who are not called, and owners who work fewer hours. This is real **change** (2.1) in the structure of work, but it is not mass replacement. The evidence in this section, however, comes mostly from firms that chose to adopt. It says less about the small businesses that did not adopt, which is where the next section turns.

## 5. The Size Gap: Uneven Adoption Reshapes Competition

### 5.1 Who adopts, and why small firms lag

Every dataset used in this essay shows that small firms adopt AI less than large ones. In the EU in 2025, 17.0% of small enterprises used AI, compared with 30.4% of medium and 55.0% of large enterprises (Eurostat, 2025). In the United States, fewer than 6% of firms used AI in 2017, but because large firms adopted far more, about 18% of employment was in firms using it (McElheran et al., 2024). The authors warn of an emerging "AI divide", a form of **digital divide** between firms rather than between individuals. This gap is not new. Before generative AI, fewer than 30% of micro-firms in Europe had adopted at least one digital technology, compared with about 80% of large firms (OECD, 2021).

The reasons match the barriers set out in Section 2. Among EU firms that considered AI but did not use it, 70.9% said they lacked the expertise (Eurostat, 2025), and the systematic review of SME studies ranks lack of knowledge and cost as the top two barriers (Oldemeyer et al., 2025). There is one important exception in the evidence. Cheap generative AI tools may be narrowing the gap: in a 2023–24 US survey, AI use among firms with one to four employees (5.5%) was close to that of firms with 250 or more (7.2%) (Bonney et al., 2024), and 31% of SMEs in seven OECD countries already used generative AI (OECD, 2025). A chatbot subscription needs no data science team. The divide may therefore be widest for the systems that need data and integration, such as machine learning and RPA, and narrowest for off-the-shelf tools.

### 5.2 The hidden cost for non-adopters

The size gap matters for employment because the firms that do not adopt are affected too. In France, when the share of competitors using robots rose by 10 percentage points, a firm's own employment fell by 2.5% (Acemoglu et al., 2020). In Spain, non-adopting firms lost about 10% of their jobs when firms using robots reached half of their industry's sales (Koch et al., 2021). The mechanism is competition: adopters cut costs and lower prices or raise quality, so they take market share from firms that did not adopt. In the US, firms investing in AI grew mainly by developing new products, and this investment was linked to rising concentration in their industries (Babina et al., 2024).

This is where the concept of **power** (2.4) becomes central. AI shifts market power towards firms that already have data, skills and money, and these are mostly large firms. For a small business, the main employment risk is therefore not that AI replaces its own workers, but that it loses customers to a larger competitor that automated first. There is an important limitation here. The clearest evidence on competition comes from manufacturing robots, not from software AI in services, so it shows the mechanism rather than proving its size for small service firms. Even so, two independent studies in two countries found the same result, which makes the mechanism credible.

## 6. Discussion: Counterarguments and Limitations

The findings so far pull in two directions, and the biggest disagreement in the evidence is about size. The experiments in Section 3 show time savings of 15 to 56%, but Danish survey data show users saving only 2.8% of their working hours and no change in earnings (Humlum & Vestergaard, 2025). At first sight one of these must be wrong. In fact they measure different things. The experiments measure one task, done with the tool, under good conditions. The surveys measure a whole working week, in which the automated task is only a small part. For the research question, the survey and firm evidence should carry more weight, because it describes small businesses as they really operate. The experiments show what is possible. The surveys show what is happening. The judgement on productivity must therefore be more modest than the headline figures suggest.

The strongest counter-argument to this essay's thesis is that small employment effects today do not mean small effects tomorrow. Frey and Osborne (2017) estimated that almost half of US jobs were at risk, and exposure estimates suggest most workers have tasks that LLMs could affect (Eloundou et al., 2024). The J-curve also predicts that the real effects arrive later, once firms reorganise (Brynjolfsson et al., 2021). This argument deserves to be taken seriously, because almost all generative AI evidence covers less than three years. However, the exposure studies measure technical possibility, not adoption, and the task-based estimate that fits small firms is far lower at about 9% (Arntz et al., 2016). It is also worth noting the standpoint of some sources. Eloundou et al. include researchers from OpenAI, and the coding trial was written mainly by staff of the tool's maker and its owner (Peng et al., 2023), so both have an interest in showing AI as powerful. The essay therefore treats future job loss as a real risk, but not as the most likely outcome on current evidence.

A second debate is whether AI helps small firms catch up or pushes them further behind. The optimistic view is that cheap generative tools give a ten-person business abilities it could not afford before, and that novices gain most (Brynjolfsson et al., 2025; OECD, 2025). The pessimistic view is that adoption and gains are concentrated in large firms, which then take market share (Babina et al., 2024; Calvino & Fontanelli, 2023). The evidence suggests both are partly right, depending on the type of system. Off-the-shelf generative tools are spreading quickly among small firms. Data-heavy machine learning and integrated automation remain mostly in large firms, and these are the systems behind the competitive effects in Section 5.

The impacts are also uneven inside small businesses. Owners benefit through lighter workloads, and less experienced staff benefit through faster learning. Workers whose routine tasks are automated pay more, especially in small firms (Bessen et al., 2025), and so do contractors who are no longer hired (OECD, 2025). This raises a question of **values and ethics** (2.7): a small firm can become more efficient while quietly shifting risk onto the people with the least security. The answer to the research question therefore depends on three conditions: the type of AI system, the owner's skill in using it, and whether competitors adopt first.

## 7. Evaluation and Conclusion

To a significant extent, AI systems that automate repetitive tasks raise productivity in small businesses at the level of the task, but so far they affect employment only to a limited extent, and mainly indirectly. Inside small firms, the evidence points to reallocation rather than replacement: tasks get faster, owners and staff work differently, and fewer new people or contractors are hired, but headcount rarely falls. The larger risk to small-business jobs comes from outside the firm. Larger competitors adopt earlier, gain more, and can take market share from small firms that do not adopt. This judgement holds under three conditions: the systems are off-the-shelf tools rather than data-heavy custom systems, the owner has the skills to use them well, and the period considered is the short term in which almost all the evidence was collected.

The main strength of this essay is its triangulation. Each key finding was checked across experiments, firm-level panel data and official business surveys, and the findings were strongest where these three agreed, for example on small current employment effects. Relying on peer-reviewed research and official statistics also meant that the method behind every figure could be checked.

The limitations are real, and each one weakens a specific part of the argument. First, no study used here directly measures the effect of AI on employment in firms with fewer than 50 employees. The essay had to combine SME surveys, firm-size splits in larger samples, and studies of large firms, so the small-business conclusion is an inference rather than a direct finding. Second, the clearest evidence on competition comes from manufacturing robots, so Section 5 shows that the mechanism exists without proving its size for small service firms. Third, the generative AI evidence covers less than three years. If the J-curve is right, the essay may describe the dip before larger effects arrive, which is why the conclusion is limited to the short term. Fourth, the surveys rely on what owners report, and owners may not notice, or may not want to admit, that AI reduced hiring. Finally, almost all the data come from high-income economies, with the Kenyan experiment as the only exception, so the findings may not transfer to countries where small firms are less digitalised.

Two questions remain open. It is not yet clear whether cheap generative AI will narrow the adoption gap between small and large firms, or whether the gap will simply move to more advanced systems. It is also unclear whether the slower hiring seen today will build into a measurable fall in small-business employment over time. The research that would settle both is a multi-year study that follows small service firms before and after adopting AI, linking business records with records of their employees' jobs and earnings. Such a study, ideally including countries outside the high-income economies, would turn this essay's inference into a direct finding.
