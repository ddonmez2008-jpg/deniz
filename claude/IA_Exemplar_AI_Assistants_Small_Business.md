## SECTION A: Inquiry Focus

### Inquiry question

**To what extent do generative AI assistants that automate repetitive office tasks affect productivity and employment in small businesses?**

### Real-world example and connection

Mehdi, Y. (2024, January 16). *Bringing the full power of Copilot to more people and businesses*. Microsoft News Center. https://news.microsoft.com/en-hk/2024/01/16/bringing-the-full-power-of-copilot-to-more-people-and-businesses/

In January 2024, Microsoft removed the 300-seat minimum for Copilot for Microsoft 365 and opened it to small businesses on its Business Standard and Business Premium plans, from one seat at US$30 per user per month (Mehdi, 2024). Copilot is a generative AI assistant built into everyday office software. It sorts email in Outlook, drafts documents in Word, analyses data in Excel and summarises meetings in Teams, drawing on the firm's own emails, chats and files (Mehdi, 2024). Before this change, the tool was mainly used by large organisations: its early-access programme included 40% of the Fortune 100. The launch therefore put the same task-automation system within reach of a ten-person firm. The announcement promised productivity but reported no measured results, and said nothing about jobs. This gap leads directly to my question: whether automating routine office tasks makes small businesses more productive, and whether it changes how many people they need.

### Context connection

The context is economic (4.2). A small business decides whether to pay for automation by comparing its cost with the labour time it saves. Owners, employees and outside contractors are affected differently: an owner may gain time while a contractor loses work. This context focuses the inquiry on measurable outcomes, output per hour and staffing, rather than on attitudes to AI.

### Content connection

The content is artificial intelligence (3.6). Copilot uses a large language model. It takes a written prompt and the firm's own data as input, predicts likely text, and outputs drafts, summaries or answers. It automates tasks that follow predictable language patterns, but it cannot judge whether its output is correct. This mechanism explains why its effect may be large on routine tasks and weak on unusual ones.

### Concept connection

The principal concept is change (2.1). Automation changes which tasks people do before it changes how many people are employed. This concept lets the inquiry separate three possible outcomes, jobs replaced, jobs reorganised or jobs unaffected, and ask which best describes small businesses so far.

## SECTION B: Sources

### Source 1 — A field study of an AI assistant in customer support

Brynjolfsson, E., Li, D., & Raymond, L. (2025). Generative AI at work. *The Quarterly Journal of Economics, 140*(2), 889–942. https://doi.org/10.1093/qje/qjae044

#### Analysis

This peer-reviewed article reports a field study of a generative AI assistant used by 5,172 customer-support agents at one software company. The authors are economists who study the productivity effects of new technology, writing for researchers and policymakers. The assistant was introduced to different agents at different times, so the same workers could be compared before and after access, and against colleagues without it. This design supports causal claims far better than a survey of users' opinions. Issues resolved per hour rose 15% on average, with gains of about 30% for less experienced agents and almost none for the most skilled (Brynjolfsson et al., 2025).

However, the setting limits my confidence. The firm is large, and the assistant learned from its own records of past customer conversations. A small business has far less data of this kind. The study also covers one highly repetitive task. Workers' own experience is largely missing, because the study measures output, not how the change felt. Together these limits mean I trust its direction of effect more than its exact size.

#### Selection and usefulness

I selected this source because it is the strongest causal evidence on how an AI assistant changes productivity on repetitive work, the first half of my question. It contributes a measured effect size and a mechanism: the system passes the methods of top workers to beginners. This matters for small firms, which often rely on staff without specialist training. It also touches the employment half, since fewer new agents quit, suggesting the tool changed the job rather than removed it. It cannot establish that a small business would see the same gain, because its data and task mix differ. I therefore treat it as an upper estimate of what is possible, and test it against Sources 2 and 3, which describe ordinary workplaces.

### Source 2 — An OECD survey of small and medium-sized enterprises

OECD. (2025). *Generative AI and the SME workforce: New survey evidence*. OECD Publishing. https://doi.org/10.1787/2d08b99d-en

#### Analysis

This report presents a representative survey of more than 5,000 SMEs in Austria, Canada, Germany, Ireland, Japan, Korea and the United Kingdom, carried out at the end of 2024. The OECD advises governments, so its purpose is to inform SME policy rather than to sell a product. Its perspective is that of owners and managers, the people who decide whether to adopt. It found that 31% of SMEs used generative AI. Among users, 83% reported no effect on staff needs, 9% a decrease and 6% an increase. About a third said it reduced workload for staff or the owner, and 14% said it reduced their use of contractors (OECD, 2025).

The large, representative sample makes it more general than a single-firm study. However, every result is a perception reported by the firm. Owners may not notice small changes, such as a vacancy they never advertised. As a single snapshot, it shows association, not causation, and employees and contractors are not asked directly. All seven countries are high-income economies with strong digital infrastructure, so the 31% adoption rate is likely to be higher than in less digitalised economies.

#### Selection and usefulness

I selected this source because it is the only one of the three that studies smaller firms directly, from the owner's perspective. It answers the employment half of my question: in most adopting SMEs, staff numbers did not change. Its most useful detail is where the change appeared instead, in lighter workloads and fewer contractors. This complements Source 1 by showing how saved time can be absorbed without job losses. Its 83% figure and Source 3's earnings results point the same way, which strengthens the employment finding more than either source alone. It also adds a stakeholder Source 1 ignores: contractors, who may lose work even when employees do not. It cannot establish the size of any productivity gain, because it measures perceptions, and the figures I accessed do not separate firms under 50 employees from medium-sized ones. This limits how precisely it answers the question for the smallest businesses.

### Source 3 — National evidence from Denmark

Humlum, A., & Vestergaard, E. (2025). *Large language models, small labor market effects* (NBER Working Paper No. 33777). National Bureau of Economic Research. https://doi.org/10.3386/w33777

#### Analysis

This working paper by two labour economists links surveys of 25,000 workers at 7,000 Danish workplaces, in eleven occupations exposed to chatbots, with official records of earnings and hours from 2023 to 2024. Comparing workers and workplaces before and after adoption allows the authors to estimate effects on real outcomes, not opinions. Their purpose is to test whether the large gains seen in experiments appear in national data. Users saved only 2.8% of their working hours on average, and adoption had no measurable effect on earnings or hours; the estimates rule out effects larger than 1% (Humlum & Vestergaard, 2025).

The link to administrative records is its main strength, because it does not depend on what firms report. However, it is not yet peer reviewed, and it has since been revised under a new title, so its figures may change. It gives no results by firm size and covers only the first two years after ChatGPT. Denmark also has high digital skills and strong worker protections, which may slow changes in staffing compared with other countries.

#### Selection and usefulness

I selected this source because it challenges Source 1. Where the field study found 15% more output, this study finds that savings across a whole working week are small and do not reach workers' pay. This tension is central to my answer: the productivity effect depends on whether it is measured on one task or across a job. It also supports Source 2's finding of little employment change, with stronger evidence, because it uses official records rather than owner perceptions. It cannot establish effects for small businesses specifically, or over the longer term, when firms may reorganise around AI. I use it to qualify the productivity claim and to argue that, so far, change in small businesses is mainly reorganisation rather than job loss.

## Appendix A: Presentation script

### 1. Inquiry focus (0:00–0:35)

> *On screen: title slide with the inquiry question; screenshot of the Microsoft announcement headline. Citation: Mehdi (2024).*

This inquiry asks: to what extent do generative AI assistants that automate repetitive office tasks affect productivity and employment in small businesses? In January 2024, Microsoft removed the 300-seat minimum for Copilot for Microsoft 365, making it available to firms buying a single licence (Mehdi, 2024). A system previously used mainly by large organisations therefore became accessible to a ten-person business. The announcement promised productivity, but it reported no measured outcomes and made no claim about employment. That gap defines the issue investigated here.

### 2. How the digital system works (0:35–1:30)

> *On screen: labelled flow diagram (original, made by me): Input (typed prompt + firm's emails, chats, files) → Processing (large language model predicts likely text) → Output (draft email, meeting summary, spreadsheet analysis) → Human check (employee accepts, edits or rejects). Side label: "Controlled by: owner buys licences and sets access; Microsoft controls the model." Citation: Mehdi (2024).*

The system combines an employee's prompt with the firm's own emails, chats and documents (Mehdi, 2024). A large language model processes these inputs by predicting probable text, and the output is a draft, a summary or an analysis. An employee then decides whether to accept, edit or reject it.

Two features of this mechanism explain the impacts examined in this presentation. Because the model predicts patterns, it performs best on standardised language tasks, such as routine replies to customers. However, it cannot verify its own accuracy, so responsibility for checking remains with a person. Control is also distributed unequally: the owner decides who receives a licence, while Microsoft determines how the model behaves. Distinguishing the model from the way a firm uses it is necessary to explain why similar tools can produce different outcomes.

### 3. Impact one: large gains on single tasks (1:30–2:50)

> *On screen: bar chart "Measured gains on routine tasks": customer support +15% issues per hour (Brynjolfsson et al., 2025); writing tasks −40% time (Noy & Zhang, 2023). Second panel: "Least experienced agents: about +30%; most experienced: close to 0".*

At the level of individual tasks, the evidence of productivity gains is strong. In a field study of more than 5,000 customer-support agents, access to an AI assistant raised issues resolved per hour by 15% (Brynjolfsson et al., 2025). In a controlled experiment, professionals using ChatGPT completed writing tasks 40% faster, with higher quality (Noy & Zhang, 2023). Because one study observed a real workplace and the other used random assignment, their agreement is more persuasive than either result alone.

The distribution of these gains is uneven. Less experienced agents improved by about 30%, while the most skilled improved very little (Brynjolfsson et al., 2025). The likely mechanism is that the system transfers the practices of high performers to beginners. For a small business that relies on inexperienced staff and cannot provide formal training, this may be the most significant effect.

Nevertheless, the gains are conditional. Consultants who used AI on a task beyond its capabilities were 19% less likely to reach a correct answer (Dell'Acqua et al., 2026). The evidence therefore supports a qualified claim: routine work becomes faster, but unusual work may deteriorate if outputs are not checked.

### 4. Impact two: a much smaller gain across the whole job (2:50–4:05)

> *On screen: two-column comparison "One task" (+15%, field study) vs "Whole working week" (2.8% of hours saved, Denmark, 25,000 workers). Citation: Humlum & Vestergaard (2025).*

Across complete jobs, the effect appears much smaller. Linking surveys of 25,000 Danish workers to official records, Humlum and Vestergaard (2025) found that chatbot users saved only 2.8% of their working hours, with no measurable change in earnings or hours worked.

These findings need not be contradictory, because they measure different outcomes. A single routine task represents only part of a working week. I give the Danish evidence greater weight for this question, because it describes ordinary jobs across thousands of workplaces rather than one task under study conditions. The productivity J-curve offers a further explanation: firms must first invest in training and new processes before gains appear (Brynjolfsson et al., 2021). Since small firms have the least capacity for that investment, their gains are likely to arrive more slowly.

### 5. Impact three: employment is reorganised, not replaced (4:05–5:25)

> *On screen: pie chart "SME users: effect on staff needs" — no effect 83%, decrease 9%, increase 6% (base: SMEs using generative AI, seven OECD countries, end of 2024; OECD, 2025). Callout: "14% use fewer contractors."*

The employment evidence points towards limited change. Among small and medium-sized firms using generative AI in an OECD survey of more than 5,000 businesses, 83% reported no effect on staff needs, 9% a decrease and 6% an increase (OECD, 2025). A US Census survey reported a similar pattern, with almost 95% of AI-using firms reporting no change in employment (Bonney et al., 2024).

The more revealing finding is where the saved time is absorbed. About a third of users reported lighter workloads, and 14% reduced their use of contractors (OECD, 2025). Stakeholder experiences consequently differ. Owners gain time, employees experience a changed role rather than a lost one, and contractors may lose work without appearing in any employment statistic. Because these figures are owners' own perceptions, they may overlook vacancies that were never advertised. However, the Danish administrative data point in the same direction, which increases my confidence in this finding.

### 6. Impact four: not every small business benefits equally (5:25–6:25)

> *On screen: bar chart "Use of AI by firm size, EU 2025": small 17.0%, medium 30.4%, large 55.0% (Eurostat, 2025). Callout: "Top barrier: lack of expertise, 70.9% of firms that considered AI."*

The benefits are also unevenly distributed between firms. In the EU in 2025, 17% of small enterprises used AI, compared with 55% of large enterprises, and 71% of firms that considered AI but did not adopt it cited a lack of expertise (Eurostat, 2025).

Capability continues to matter after adoption. When Kenyan small-business owners received a GPT-4 business assistant, high performers gained about 15%, whereas weaker performers did slightly worse (Otis et al., 2023). Since that tool provided advice rather than automating tasks, the comparison is indirect. It nevertheless suggests that outcomes depend on the owner's ability to evaluate and apply the output. Workers may also bear costs: in Dutch data, employees of firms that automated lost earnings over five years, with larger losses in smaller firms (Bessen et al., 2025).

### 7. Answer to the inquiry question (6:25–7:10)

> *On screen: answer summary slide with three lines: Productivity — large on routine tasks, small across the job; Employment — little change so far, reorganisation not replacement; Conditions — task type, owner skill, early period.*

Generative AI assistants substantially increase productivity on routine tasks, but across complete jobs the measured gain is so far small. Their effect on employment is limited: in small businesses, the change has mainly taken the form of reorganisation, through lighter workloads and reduced use of contractors, rather than job losses. The strongest support for this judgement comes from the Danish administrative data combined with the OECD survey. Its principal limitation is time. Most of the evidence covers only the first two years of adoption in high-income economies, so the conclusion applies to small businesses in those settings, in the short term.

### 8. Emerging trends and future developments (7:10–9:20)

> *On screen: section title "Emerging trends and future developments".*

**Development 1: adoption among small firms is accelerating.**

> *On screen: line chart "EU enterprises using AI": 8.0% (2023), 13.5% (2024), 20.0% (2025) (Eurostat, 2025). Label: "measured, official statistics".*

The first development is the pace of adoption. The share of EU enterprises using AI rose from 8% in 2023 to 20% in 2025 (Eurostat, 2025). This is a measured trend rather than a forecast. Its significance is that the main effect on a small business may increasingly come from competitors rather than from its own use of AI. Studies of robot adoption in France and Spain found that firms which did not automate lost employment when their competitors did (Acemoglu et al., 2020; Koch et al., 2021). Because that evidence concerns manufacturing robots rather than office software, it identifies a plausible mechanism rather than an established effect. If the mechanism applies, however, the employment risk for small businesses shifts from replacement to competition.

**Development 2: AI agents that complete whole workflows.**

> *On screen: "Assistant vs agent" diagram: assistant drafts one email; agent reads the request, updates the spreadsheet, emails the client. Result box: "Best agent completed 24% of tasks in a simulated small software company" (Xu et al., 2024; preprint). Label: "measured benchmark, not real firms".*

The second development is the shift from assistants to agents, which complete several steps without human input, such as reading a request, updating a spreadsheet and replying to a client. Technology companies present agents as transformative, but an announcement is not evidence of performance. In a benchmark simulating a small software company, the most capable agent completed only 24% of tasks autonomously (Xu et al., 2024). The study is a preprint and tests a simulation rather than real businesses, so it cannot predict workplace outcomes. It does, however, indicate that agents are not yet reliable enough to replace complete roles. If that rate improves substantially, the human check in my system diagram could be removed. Reorganisation would then become a less accurate description, and my judgement about employment would need to change.

### 9. Closing insight (9:20–9:40)

> *On screen: final slide "What would change my judgement?"*

The future-facing evidence shows that the current limited effect on employment depends on AI still requiring human verification. This judgement would change if agents became reliable enough to work unsupervised, or if long-term data showed small firms losing ground to competitors that adopted earlier. Thank you.
