## Presentation sources (all already in the IPD reference list)

- Wang, Wang & Liu (2026): real-world example. Writing scores 77.13 → 81.40, flow 3.15 → 3.60, motivation 2.93 → 3.00 (n.s.).
- Heung & Chiu (2025): meta-analysis, 17 studies, 1,735 students, medium effect on overall engagement.
- Chan & Wong (2025): GPT-4 literary analysis (Hamlet, The Great Gatsby, Oliver Twist), marked by 4 university teachers. Analysing 82%, evaluating 71%, depth weakest (67%).
- Fryer et al. (2017): 122 students, 12 weeks. Task interest with a chatbot partner dropped after the first task (novelty effect); only the human partner built course interest.
- Wu & Yu (2024): 24 randomised studies, large effect. Short interventions were stronger, and the effect was greater in higher education than in K-12.
- OpenAI (29 July 2025): study mode runs on custom system instructions (an announcement, not evidence).
- Bastani et al. (2025, PNAS): about 1,000 Turkish high school maths students. Practice +48% (standard) and +127% (guardrailed); exam −17% (standard) and no loss (guardrailed).
- Oreopoulos & Low (2026, NBER WP 35620): 18 Tennessee middle schools over 2 years. 96% tried Khanmigo; the median student messaged it on a third of practice days and in 17% of sessions after a mistake; gains were similar to Khan Academy without AI.
- OECD (2026), Digital Education Outlook: general chatbots raise performance without learning gains; pedagogical tools show sustained improvements; 72% of lower secondary teachers are worried about integrity (TALIS 2024).

## 0:00 · Inquiry focus (s01)

To what extent do generative AI chatbots such as ChatGPT influence the engagement of high school students in online English literature classes? In Taiwan, forty ninth-graders used ChatGPT as a literary assistant during a three-day writing camp. According to Wang and colleagues, their writing scores rose by about four points, and they were more absorbed in the task. But their motivation to write did not change. Some students said that when the chatbot did too much, the task lost its challenge. That gap, between doing better and wanting to learn, is what this inquiry investigates, through human knowledge, artificial intelligence, and the concept of power.

## 0:42 · How the digital system works (s02)

First, how the system works. The input is the student's prompt, for example: explain the green light in The Great Gatsby. Hidden instructions from the platform or the school are added to it. A large language model then processes this by predicting the most probable next words. The output is fluent text: a summary, an interpretation, even a full paragraph. The student decides what to do with it. But the vendor, the school and the teacher control the hidden instructions. That is the key part of the mechanism. The same model can hand over a finished reading of a text, or ask the student a question back. So the impact on engagement should depend on how the chatbot is set up, and on who sets it up.

## 1:27 · Theme 1: Activity or engagement? (s03)

Theme one: are students more engaged, or just more active? According to Source A, Lo and colleagues' review of seventy-two studies, the strongest evidence is behavioural: students spend time working with ChatGPT. But in the same studies, the main form of disengagement was academic dishonesty. According to Heung and Chiu's meta-analysis of seventeen studies, ChatGPT-based learning had a medium positive effect on overall engagement. So on average, activity goes up. But activity is not the same as engaging with a text. For example, a student can spend twenty minutes prompting a chatbot about Hamlet and never form a reading of their own.

## 2:09 · Theme 1: Qualified (s04)

This needs qualifying. According to Source A, the evidence on cognitive engagement is broad but weak. Some studies show better understanding, others show over-reliance. Literature makes this sharper. According to Chan and Wong, when four university teachers marked GPT-4's analysis of Hamlet, The Great Gatsby and Oliver Twist, it scored eighty-two percent for analysing, but only seventy-one percent for evaluating, and depth was its weakest area. So the chatbot is good at the part of literature study that sounds finished, and weaker at judgement. If a student accepts its reading, the task looks done. But the interpretive work, the part that is assessed, has been handed over. This links back to human knowledge: whose reading is it?

*Footage:* finger_reading mirrored (13805), typing (1808)

## 2:58 · Theme 2: Motivation and support (s05)

Theme two: motivation, and who structures the interaction. According to Source B, Chiu and colleagues randomly assigned one hundred and twenty-three Grade ten students learning English as a second language to use a chatbot, with or without teacher support. Teacher support raised students' sense of relatedness, but lowered their sense of autonomy. Students with low self-regulation or low digital literacy gained motivation from teacher support, while the most independent students did better without it. So the same tool motivates some students and leaves others behind. In an online literature class, where the teacher is less present, the students who most need structure may be the least likely to get it.

*Footage:* girl_library (36068), typing_detail mirrored (4907)

## 3:41 · Theme 2: The students' perspective (s06)

The students' own perspective comes from Source C, Pew's survey of one thousand three hundred and ninety-one U.S. teens. According to Source C, the share using ChatGPT for schoolwork doubled, from thirteen percent in twenty twenty-three to twenty-six percent in twenty twenty-four. But teens drew a line. Fifty-four percent said it was acceptable for researching a topic, but only eighteen percent for writing an essay, and forty-two percent said that was not acceptable. That matters for literature, which is mostly assessed by essay. So many students are uneasy about the very use that could replace their own reading. Source C measures attitudes, not engagement. But it shows that students are not simply passive users.

*Footage:* students_walk (28320), teen_reading_lamp mirrored (50128)

## 4:28 · Theme 3: Does engagement last? (s07)

Theme three: does engagement last? All of the studies in Source A were published within a year of ChatGPT's release, and Source B lasted six sessions. According to Fryer and colleagues, in a twelve-week language course, students' interest in tasks with a chatbot partner dropped after the first task. With a human partner it did not, and only the human partner built interest in the course itself. According to Wu and Yu's meta-analysis of twenty-four randomised studies, chatbots had a large effect on learning. But short interventions had stronger effects than long ones, and the effect was stronger in higher education than in schools. So part of the engagement in my sources may simply be novelty.

*Footage:* typing_fast mirrored (42748)

## 5:13 · Weighing the evidence (s07b)

So which evidence should carry the most weight? Source B is the strongest on cause, because of random assignment, and it matches my age group. But its chatbot was not a large language model, and the task was English as a second language, not literature. Source A gives the widest picture, but most of its studies used self-report and university students. Source C has the best sample of teenagers, but it measures use and attitudes, not engagement. So I give Source B the most weight for how engagement works, Source A for what type of engagement changes, and Source C for who is affected. None of them studies online literature classes directly, which limits my answer.

*Footage:* boy_library mirrored (4510)

## 5:57 · Answer to the inquiry question (s08)

So, to answer my question. Generative AI chatbots appear to raise behavioural engagement in the short term: students do more with the tool. But for high school students in online English literature classes, the effect on deeper engagement is mixed. Where it hands over a finished reading, it can replace the interpretive work that literature study is about. Where a teacher structures the interaction, less independent students gain motivation. The strongest evidence for this is Source B, because it is experimental. The main limitation is that no source tests a generative chatbot in an online literature class, and the evidence covers only the first months of use. So the influence is moderate, and conditional on design, for now.

*Footage:* teen_reading_lamp (50128), boy_library (4510)

## 6:42 · D1: Guardrailed learning modes (s09)

Emerging trends and future developments. The first development is the shift to guardrailed learning modes. According to OpenAI, study mode was launched in July twenty twenty-five to guide students instead of giving answers, and it runs on custom system instructions. That is an announcement, not evidence. The measured evidence comes from Bastani and colleagues, with high school students in Turkey. According to their results, students with a standard ChatGPT tutor scored forty-eight percent higher in practice, but seventeen percent lower on the exam once it was taken away. A tutor with guardrails raised practice scores by one hundred and twenty-seven percent, without that loss. That was mathematics, not literature. But it suggests the hidden instructions in my system diagram could decide whether a chatbot supports engagement or replaces it. And students can still switch the mode off.

*Footage:* hands_laptop mirrored (4763), finger_reading (13805)

## 7:37 · D2: Access is not engagement (s10)

The second development is schools adopting tutor-style chatbots at scale. Oreopoulos and Low ran a two-year randomised trial in eighteen Tennessee middle schools using Khanmigo, which coaches instead of giving answers. According to their results, ninety-six percent of students tried it. But the median student messaged it on only a third of the days they practised, and in only seventeen percent of the sessions where they made a mistake. Achievement gains were small, and similar to Khan Academy without AI. This is a working paper, in maths, with younger students. If a guardrailed chatbot refuses to do the work, many students simply stop using it. So the real limit may be engagement itself, not access.

*Footage:* students_walk mirrored (28320), typing mirrored (1808)

## 8:23 · D3: Teachers set the instructions (s11)

The third development is policy moving towards educational AI rather than general chatbots. According to the OECD's Digital Education Outlook twenty twenty-six, general-purpose chatbots can raise task performance without real learning gains, while tools designed with a clear teaching purpose tend to show sustained improvements. The same report found that seventy-two percent of lower secondary teachers believe AI can harm academic integrity. This is a synthesis of evidence, not a new trial, and it repeats some of the studies I have used. But it points to a shift in power. If schools and teachers, not vendors, set the instructions, then the factor Source B found most important, who structures the interaction, moves back to the teacher.

*Footage:* typing_detail (4907)

## 9:10 · Closing insight (s12)

So what does the future-facing evidence add? It shows that the real question is not whether chatbots engage students, but who designs that engagement, and whether students accept it. My judgement would change if a randomised trial in online literature classes measured students' own interpretations over a full year. There is also an equity question. According to Source C, awareness of ChatGPT was eighty-four percent in households earning seventy-five thousand dollars or more, but sixty-seven percent in households under thirty thousand. So any benefit could reach some students first. That is the evidence I would look for next.
