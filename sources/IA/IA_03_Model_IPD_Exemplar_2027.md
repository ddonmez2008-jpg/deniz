Section A Inquiry focus
Inquiry question
To what extent can generative AI tutoring improve independent mathematics learning for secondary school
students in Turkey?

Real-world example and connection
In autumn 2023, nearly 1,000 students in grades 9–11 at a Turkish high school participated in an
experiment using GPT-4 mathematics tutors. Across four 90-minute sessions, classes used GPT Base, GPT
Tutor or their usual textbooks and notes. GPT Base provided conversational assistance, while GPT Tutor was
instructed to give hints and supplied with teacher-prepared solutions and guidance on common mistakes.
Students practised with their assigned resources before taking closed-book, closed-laptop examinations.
Both AI groups improved their assisted practice scores. However, students using GPT Base subsequently
scored 17% lower than the control group when assistance was removed. GPT Tutor’s unaided performance
was not statistically distinguishable from the control. These contrasting outcomes connect the school’s use
of AI tutoring directly to students’ ability to solve mathematics independently (Bastani et al., 2025,
Experimental Design and Main Results).

Context connection
The context is human knowledge (4.5). Completing a task and acquiring knowledge are different
educational outcomes. The inquiry therefore evaluates evidence of understanding alongside the benefits of
accessible help. Studies from other settings provide comparisons and possible future directions; they do
not establish effects in Turkey by themselves.

Content connection
The content connection is artificial intelligence (3.6). The inquiry examines how a language model, its
instructions and supplied mathematical material produce different forms of feedback. Distinguishing the
model from the tutoring application is necessary to explain why systems using similar technology can have
different educational effects.

Concept connection
The principal concept is power (2.4). A system designer can decide whether a learner receives a complete
solution, a hint or a question. Teachers also exercise power through the tasks and assessment rules they
select. This connection makes it possible to investigate who controls the intellectual work and whether
students gain the capacity to act independently.

Section B Sources
Source 1 The Turkish high school experiment
Bastani, H., Bastani, O., Sungu, A., Ge, H., Kabakcı, Ö., & Mariman, R. (2025). Generative AI without guardrails can
harm learning: Evidence from high school mathematics. Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences, 122(26),
e2422633122.
https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2422633122

Analysis
This peer-reviewed research article is the central source because its population, subject and setting most
closely match the question. Its authors combine expertise in computing and educational applications of AI.
Their purpose is to test educational consequences, addressing researchers and educators. That expertise
makes the system comparison interpretable, but the strength of the inquiry depends on the research
design rather than the journal name.

The study compared classroom groups, preregistered its analysis and used independent grading. It found
that unrestricted assistance could undermine later unaided performance, while the guarded tutor did not
demonstrate an independent-learning advantage over the control (Bastani et al., 2025, Experimental Design
and Main Results). These features make it more useful for causal reasoning than a survey asking students
whether AI helped them. Preregistration also makes selective emphasis on attractive outcomes easier to
scrutinize.

Nevertheless, a result in one school cannot establish a national effect. The short assessment horizon
restricts conclusions about durable understanding. Moreover, the guarded design bundled restrictions on
answers with teacher-supplied mathematical guidance. Consequently, any difference between designs
cannot be attributed solely to withholding answers. This matters because a school might copy the
restriction while omitting the supporting content and then expect the same result.

Selection and usefulness
This source is selected as the inquiry’s principal account of how the system was designed and tested.
Because the researchers developed the intervention, their explanation helps connect application settings to
outcomes, while also requiring scrutiny of their outcome definitions. I use it to establish the mechanism
and the local comparison, rather than treating the developers’ interpretation as a sufficient basis for
adoption.

I give this source greatest weight when answering the question for the Turkish case. It establishes the need
to separate supported task completion from learning and supplies the comparison against which optimistic
claims can be tested. Its limitation changes my conclusion: safeguards can be defended as a way of reducing
a demonstrated risk, but this study alone cannot justify claiming that a safeguarded tutor improves
independent learning.

Source 2 A comparison with structured AI instruction
Kestin, G., Miller, K., Klales, A., Milbourne, T., & Ponti, G. (2025). AI tutoring outperforms in-class active learning: An
RCT introducing a novel research-based design in an authentic educational setting. Scientific Reports, 15, 17458.
https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-025-97652-6

Analysis
Kestin and colleagues are physics educators and researchers reporting a peer-reviewed classroom
experiment. Their purpose is to test a tutor built around educational principles against an established
teaching approach, for an audience concerned with learning and instructional design. As developers of the
tutor, they have an intellectual investment in its success. That perspective makes the comparison and
assessment design especially important to examine.

The randomized crossover study involved 194 undergraduate physics students at Harvard. Learners
experienced a structured AI lesson and an active-learning class across two topics, with pre-tests and
post-tests. The tutor condition produced higher immediate scores (Kestin et al., 2025, Results and
Methods). An active-learning comparison is valuable: it tests the intervention against purposeful instruction
rather than an unusually weak alternative.

The crossover approach reduces some differences between groups because students encounter both
conditions. However, the intervention also changes the learning environment, including self-pacing and
studying at home. I therefore interpret it as evidence about a designed educational package, rather than a
clean estimate of the language model alone. A school cannot assume that adding a chatbot to existing
homework reproduces that package.

Selection and usefulness
Its most serious limitation for my question is transferability. University physics students differ from Turkish
secondary mathematics learners in age, prior knowledge and institutional setting. Immediate post-tests
also leave later retention unresolved. These differences do not make the study irrelevant; they change the
role it can reasonably play. It can challenge an assertion that generative AI necessarily damages learning,
but it cannot supply a Turkish effect size.

I use this source as a counterweight to an overly negative interpretation of the central case. Comparing the
studies directs attention to scaffolding, assessment and context. Their different findings need not be
contradictory because the interventions and outcomes differ. The source therefore strengthens a
conditional answer: structured tutoring may support learning, while the magnitude and durability of any
benefit for the defined Turkish group remain unproven. I would need a comparable local trial before
recommending implementation on the strength of this study alone.

Source 3 AI support delivered through a human tutor
Wang, R. E., Ribeiro, A. T., Robinson, C. D., Loeb, S., & Demszky, D. (2026). Tutor CoPilot: A human-AI approach for
scaling real-time expertise (Version 3) [Preprint; first posted 2024]. arXiv.
https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.03017v3

Analysis
This research preprint investigates a different distribution of responsibility: AI supports a human tutor
instead of directly replacing the tutor’s response to a student. The authors are researchers in computing
and education at Stanford. Their purpose is to investigate whether expert guidance can be extended
through a combined human and AI system. I evaluate the full October 2026 version, rather than treating its
earlier abstract as the definitive account.

The revised report analyzes 783 tutors and 1,013 students. Tutors were randomized to access or no access;
students could encounter both conditions. It reports an approximately four-percentage-point improvement
in session topic mastery, without a statistically significant end-of-year mathematics improvement (Wang et
al., 2026, Methods, Results and Limitations). The distinction between session and annual outcomes
prevents an immediate benefit from being presented as established long-term progress.

Preregistration supports scrutiny of the intended analysis. However, the assignment unit matters:
randomizing tutors does not mean that each student receives a stable treatment throughout the study. This
limits what the evidence can establish about sustained exposure. The report is also a preprint in the version
examined, so I do not assume that arXiv hosting supplies journal peer review.

Selection and usefulness
Transferability is restricted by the younger American population and the intervention’s dependence on a
tutoring provider. A Turkish school might lack comparable staffing, language support or infrastructure. The
authors’ interest in the system’s educational promise is a reason to distinguish measured outcomes from
proposed wider benefits. A plausible account of scale is not itself evidence that implementation will be
affordable or effective locally.

This source is most useful for developing the inquiry beyond a simple choice between allowing and banning
student chatbots. It identifies a way that teachers could retain authority over assistance while drawing on
generated suggestions. In comparison with the other studies, it shifts attention to who mediates the
feedback. I use it to justify investigating supervised designs, not to claim that human oversight guarantees
independent learning or that a national attainment gap has already been reduced.
