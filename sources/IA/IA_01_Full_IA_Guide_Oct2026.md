How to use this guide
A practical synthesis of both webinars, with the repetition removed.

A strong IA makes a focused, evidence-based argument about how a digital system
affects people and communities. The format helps the examiner find that
argument; the quality of the thinking earns the marks.

Three kinds of guidance
Webinar-stated requirement. A limit or expectation explicitly presented as compulsory in the
transcript. Check the current official guide and session procedures before submission.

Presenter recommendation. Advice intended to make the work stronger or easier to assess.
Examples include the 300/1,200-word split, captions and choosing one main connection for each of
the three Cs.

Practical framework. The templates, worked examples, time plan and checklists created for this
guide. They are teaching aids, not official IB forms or markband wording.

The assessment package
Same project at SL and HL. The webinars state a total of 24 marks, contributing 30% of the final
subject result at SL and 20% at HL.

Written limit: 1,500 words. This is the combined limit for Sections A and B of the Inquiry Process
Document (IPD). Work beyond the limit is not credited.

Recorded limit: 10 minutes. The multimedia presentation covers C, D and E. Content after 10:00
is not credited. A separate script does not extend the time available.

Three evaluated sources. Section B contains exactly three sources. The wider inquiry and
presentation should draw on additional relevant research.

  The change to remember
  Finish the analysis with a conclusion that answers the inquiry question under Criterion C. Then
  clearly begin Criterion D: Emerging trends and future developments. Do not use the whole final
  section to repeat the main conclusion.

These descriptions are concise paraphrases of the webinars, not a replacement for the full official assessment
descriptors. No template guarantees a particular mark or final subject grade.

  FORMAT & WORD BUDGET

Build the submission in this order
A clear structure gives each criterion an obvious home.

  Component  Recommended organisation

  IPD: Section A  Inquiry question → real-world example and its connection → context → content
  → concept. The order of the three Cs is flexible.

  IPD: Section B  Source 1: reference, analysis, usefulness.
  Source 2: reference, analysis, usefulness.
  Source 3: reference, analysis, usefulness.

  List of references  All sources and materials used across both the IPD and the presentation,
  including media and acknowledged tools.

  Appendices, if needed  Primary-research evidence, relevant supporting material, AI-use records and,
  optionally, the presentation script.

  Recorded presentation  Question → digital system → analysis/evaluation → conclusion answering the
  question → trends/future developments → closing insight.

A workable allocation
Section A: about 300 words. Section B: about 1,200 words. This is a recommendation, not
two separate maximums. If A is 350 words, B must be no more than 1,150. A useful starting point
for B is about 400 words per source: roughly half analysis and half justification.

The webinars exclude citations and the reference list from the word count. Part 2 also says subtitles
do not count. Keep substantive discussion within A and B; do not disguise analysis as an oversized
heading, footnote or appendix to evade the limit.

Keep the assessed sections easy to find
Use clear headings, readable type and black body text. A project title is optional; the inquiry
question is essential. There is no page limit stated in the webinars, and Part 2 accepts 1.15 or 1.5
line spacing. Those choices do not earn marks by themselves.

  A safe layout principle
  Put the three-C connections in Section A and the source analyses in Section B. In the
  presentation, use the research to build an argument; do not read Sections A and B aloud.

  CRITERION A • 3 MARKS

Choose a question you can answer
The scope must fit the evidence, the word limit and the recording.

A useful question names four things
A specific digital system + an effect or issue + a defined group + a manageable context.
Not every question needs a school or city, but the boundaries must be clear enough for a
convincing investigation.

  Too broad  More focused teaching example

  How does social media affect  How does Instagram influence the online self-presentation of
  teenagers?  students aged 16-18 at School X?

  Is AI good for education?  To what extent does the use of one named AI feedback tool
  influence revision practices among Year 12 English students at
  School X?

  What are the benefits of digital  To what extent does a named mobile-payment system improve
  payments?  access to payments for a defined group of traders in one location?

These are illustrative question forms, not pre-approved IA titles. The student must establish that suitable real-world
evidence exists and that the proposed investigation is feasible.

Test the focus before approving it
Can the technology be explained? A label such as “technology” or “AI” may hide too many
systems. Specify the system, application or feature that matters.

Can the people and setting be identified? Avoid treating all teenagers, countries or industries
as if they experience the same effects.

Can contrasting evidence be obtained? A question with a predetermined answer limits
evaluation. “Influence” can be useful when “improve” assumes a benefit, although the webinar
does not prescribe one compulsory wording.

Can the evidence support the claim? A small self-report survey cannot establish that an app
causes sleep loss. Investigate perceptions or associations, or draw on suitable published research
and expert evidence.

  Local is useful, not mandatory
  Local settings often make primary research and precise conclusions easier. They are a presenter
  recommendation, not a requirement to conduct the IA in the student’s own school or city.

  CRITERION A • WRITING TEMPLATE

Connect the example and the three Cs
Explain relationships instead of listing syllabus terms.

  Suggested heading  What to write

  Inquiry question  State the final question clearly and consistently.

  Real-world example and  Identify a specific event, implementation or documented case. Cite its
  connection  source at the point of use. Explain how it leads to this inquiry.

  Context connection  Name the most relevant context and explain why this setting or social
  situation matters to the question.

  Content connection  Identify the relevant digital content and explain how the system or
  mechanism is involved.

  Concept connection  Identify the most useful conceptual lens and explain what it helps the
  inquiry examine.

What a connection actually sounds like
Illustrative focus: Instagram and online self-presentation among students in a defined school
community.

Context - social. The inquiry examines how peer expectations within the school community shape
what students choose to publish and how they respond to one another.

Content - media. The inquiry considers how image-based posts, visible feedback and platform
features influence the construction and circulation of students’ public profiles.

Concept - identity. The inquiry explores whether students’ curated profiles reinforce or differ
from how they understand themselves offline.
These short examples illustrate the connection move. They are not a complete Section A and do not supply the
required real-world example or research evidence.

Make the limited space work harder
The presenters recommend developing one principal context, one principal content area and
one principal concept in depth. Naming several of each can leave only a sentence for every
connection. Additional relevant concepts can still appear in the presentation.

Part 1 recommends accurate topic and subtopic labels or numbers from the current guide. A code
or definition does not replace an explanation of the connection.

  The real-world example must be real
  A description of AI is only a topic. Part 1 accepts personal experience but recommends a
  verifiable published case where possible.

  CRITERION B • 6 MARKS

Analyse and justify three sources
Source quality matters because of what it allows you to investigate.

For each source, the examiner should understand what it claims, how much
confidence to place in it, and exactly why it is useful for this inquiry. A
summary alone leaves important work undone.

Repeat this structure for Sources 1, 2 and 3
  Element  What to include

  1. Source heading  Source number and a clear title or source description.

  2. Full reference  A complete, consistently formatted bibliographic entry. Repeat the entry
  in the final list of references.

  3. Analysis  Relevant claims and perspectives; author or organisation; purpose;
  About 200 words  evidence or method; reliability; limitations and possible bias.

  4. Selection and usefulness  The specific contribution to the inquiry, the aspect it helps answer, and
  About 200 words  the way it supports, challenges or complements other evidence.

Questions that deepen the analysis
Claims and perspective. What does the source argue or report? Whose interests or experiences
are represented? What relevant voices are absent?

Evidence and reliability. Does it explain how its data were obtained? Does expertise match the
claim? Can a key finding be checked against another source?

Purpose and limitations. Was it produced to inform, persuade, market a product or influence
policy? How might the audience, funding, sample or date restrict its conclusions?

Usefulness. Which part of the inquiry will this source support? What will it help explain that the
other sources do not? What can it not establish?

  Protect the word budget
  Section B does not need a general introduction. Begin with Source 1. Do not add a fourth
  evaluated source here: the webinars specify exactly three. Put wider research to work in the
  presentation and reference list.

Source: Part 1 pp. 12-15, 27, 29; Part 2 p. 39.

  CRITERION B • STRONGER REASONING

Judge each source’s contribution
Demonstrate why a source is valuable and where its value ends.

  Weak comment  A stronger analytical move

  “The author is an expert, so the  Identify the relevant expertise, inspect the method or evidence,
  source is reliable.”  and explain which claims that expertise supports.

  “This source is biased.”  Identify a specific interest or imbalance and explain how it affects
  the interpretation. Bias does not automatically make every detail
  unusable.

  “It relates to my question.”  Name the sub-question it helps answer and the evidence it
  supplies for that purpose.

  “The source agrees with Source 1.”  Explain what they agree about, whether their evidence is
  independent and what the agreement adds to the inquiry.

  “It is recent.”  Explain why its date matters for this system or issue; a recent
  source may still have weak evidence.

Illustrative source-analysis paragraph
“The provider’s report explains the tool’s feedback process and reports positive user experiences.
Its technical detail makes it useful for understanding the intended operation of the system.
However, the provider benefits commercially from adoption, and the report does not establish
whether the reported users represent the wider student population. Its claims about learning
outcomes therefore need comparison with independent evidence.”

Illustrative usefulness paragraph
“I will use this source to explain the system’s intended benefits and the provider’s perspective. I
will compare those claims with independent research and students’ accounts of how they revise
their work. This comparison will help assess the gap between the advertised function and actual
use. The source cannot, on its own, demonstrate improved attainment at my school.”

  What makes the second paragraph useful?
  It specifies a role for the source, links that role to the inquiry, plans a comparison and sets a
  boundary on the conclusion. These are model reasoning moves; students must replace the
  hypothetical evidence with their own verified research.

Selection principle: choose three sources that make distinct, relevant contributions. Three similar articles repeating
the same announcement may provide less analytical range than sources with different evidence, methods or
perspectives.

  RESEARCH FOUNDATION

Give primary research a purpose
It strengthens an inquiry only when its method and relevance are sound.

  Primary research is not compulsory
  The webinars encourage it but do not make it a requirement for top marks. There is no
  prescribed “two secondary plus one primary” formula. A primary source earns no automatic
  credit simply for being primary.

A productive research sequence
1. Compare the published claims and identify a question or gap.
2. Decide whether an interview, survey or other feasible investigation can address it.
3. Analyse the findings, including their limitations.
4. Compare those findings with the published evidence and explain agreement or difference.

  If you use…  Document and show…

  An interview  The interviewee’s role or relevant expertise, date, method, questions and a
  transcript of responses. Cite the interview where its evidence is used.

  A survey  Who was approached, how participants were selected, how and when it ran,
  the questions, number of responses, results and relevant limitations or bias.

  A percentage  The denominator as well as the percentage. “75%” means very different
  things with 4 responses and with 400.

  Your own images or  What they show, how they relate to the argument and that they are
  clips  student-created. Include supporting details where necessary.

The presentation needs the essential method too
Do not leave every methodological detail in an appendix. Briefly establish who was studied, how
the evidence was obtained and the main limitation before relying on the findings. The appendix
supplies the fuller record.

Neither webinar gives a universal minimum survey sample. The number must be justified by the
inquiry’s scope and the claims made. A convenience sample of classmates can describe those
respondents; it does not automatically represent all young people.
Practical recommendation: obtain appropriate consent, minimise identifying information and follow the school’s
research procedures, especially with minors. Avoid collecting sensitive information that the inquiry does not need.

What this timing protects
Criterion D is worth the same six marks as Criterion C. The presenters recommend about 2-2.5
minutes for D, so it should not be a hurried final slide. The plan above reserves 2.5 minutes
including the closing insight.

The hard limit is 10 minutes; there is no stated minimum. Part 1 recommends more than nine
minutes for depth, but a shorter recording does not attract an automatic penalty.

Use headings that reveal the argument
Examples: “How the system works”, “Impact on students”, “Contrasting evidence”, “Answer to the
inquiry question”, and “Emerging trends and future developments”. Within the analysis, use
headings that say what the section examines rather than only “Source 1” or “Source 2”.

  No compulsory slide count
  No slide count or application is prescribed. Choose screens that explain the argument, then
  rehearse the complete export at a natural pace.

  MULTIMEDIA • DIGITAL SYSTEM

Explain how the technology works
The examiner needs the mechanism, not a product advertisement.

Part 2 identifies a recurring omission: students discuss the social issue but fail to
explain the digital technology. Give the audience the technical understanding
needed for the later analysis.

  Explain  Ask yourself

  Input  What data or signals enter the system? Where do they come from?

  Processing  What relevant computational process transforms those inputs?

  Output  What recommendation, classification, decision, response or action results?

  People and control  Who uses the output? Who decides the settings or has access to the data?

  Connection to the issue  Which part of that mechanism produces the impact or raises the concern
  being investigated?

An illustrative explanation
For an AI writing-feedback tool, identify what student text is submitted, how the named tool
generates feedback and what the student sees. Explain whether the feedback is used to revise
work and which data-handling features are relevant to the inquiry. Verify the details for the actual
product: do not assume every AI tool works or stores data in the same way.

Choose a visual that does explanatory work
A labelled process diagram, an annotated interface or a short relevant demonstration can make the
mechanism understandable. Refer to the specific part shown while speaking. Cite the technical
documentation, research or other evidence supporting the explanation.

Build a storyboard before recording
  For each screen, record…  So that…

  Main claim + supporting evidence  The screen contributes to the inquiry.

  Visual + spoken explanation  Image and narration make the same point.

  Citation + full-reference location  The claim and media are traceable.

  Duration + transition  The argument fits within the total time.

  CRITERION C • 6 MARKS

Move from information to evaluation
Strong analysis explains effects; strong evaluation weighs their significance.

  Thinking move  What it adds

  Description  Identifies what the system does or what a source reports.

  Analysis  Explains how or why an effect occurs, who experiences it and how it relates to the
  question.

  Evaluation  Judges importance, credibility, extent, trade-offs and conditions using evidence.

  Synthesis  Combines findings into an overall understanding rather than leaving each source
  in isolation.

A repeatable analytical sequence
1. Make a focused claim. State one impact relevant to the inquiry, not a general opinion about
technology.

2. Provide evidence. Use a finding, documented case, interview extract or result. Cite it at the
point of use.

3. Explain the mechanism. Show how a feature of the digital system helps produce this effect.

4. Identify the people affected. Explain who benefits, who may be disadvantaged and why
experiences differ.

5. Weigh a qualification or counterpoint. Compare another source, identify a limitation or show
a condition under which the effect changes.

6. Reach a reasoned judgement. Explain what the evidence permits you to conclude about the
inquiry question.

What “sustained” means in practice
Analysis should continue across the presentation. Do not give several minutes of description and
add one evaluative sentence at the end. Develop initial claims with further evidence, comparisons
and judgements as the argument progresses.

  Use the sources differently from Section B
  In B, analyse the sources and justify their selection. In C, use their evidence to analyse the
  effects of the digital system on people and communities. Briefly qualifying a source can help,
  but repeating its full B evaluation wastes time.

Depth matters more than a long list of effects. Organise the presentation around a manageable set of issues that can
be properly investigated and compared.

  CRITERION C • WORKED EXAMPLE

Build a reasoned conclusion
An illustrative structure, using hypothetical evidence rather than invented findings.

Example focus
To what extent does a named AI feedback tool influence revision practices among Year 12 English
students at School X?

  Stage  Example of the reasoning move

  Description  The tool gives students feedback on submitted writing.

  Analysis  Immediate feedback could allow revision before the next lesson. If the students’
  evidence shows that they act on that feedback, the mechanism helps explain
  changes in revision timing.

  Evaluation  More revisions do not necessarily mean better learning. Compare what students
  say with appropriate evidence about the quality of the changes and their ability to
  explain them.

  Qualification  A small volunteer sample may overrepresent confident users. Self-reported
  usefulness cannot establish improved examination performance.

  Judgement  A defensible conclusion might concern changes in reported revision habits. A
  claim about improved attainment would require stronger and different evidence.

What belongs in the conclusion of C
Answer the actual question. State the extent or nature of the effect, identify the most
persuasive evidence, weigh the principal qualification and respect the limits of the population or
setting studied.

A useful structure is: “Overall, the evidence indicates [answer] for [defined group], mainly because
[supported reason]. However, [counter-evidence or limitation] means that this conclusion applies
most convincingly when [condition].”

Use this as a thinking scaffold, not a sentence to complete mechanically. The conclusion must
follow the actual findings. It should not introduce a major unsupported claim or pretend that
uncertainty has disappeared.

  Make the transition visible
  Finish the answer to the inquiry question. Then introduce a new heading: “Emerging trends and
  future developments”. Criterion D develops researched further insight; it does not substitute for
  the missing conclusion of C.

All examples on this page are instructional and hypothetical. They make no claim that an actual tool, school,
interview or study produced these findings.

  CRITERION D • 6 MARKS

Research what may come next
Future-facing discussion still needs evidence.

Criterion D needs a well-supported discussion related to the inquiry focus. “In the
future, AI will improve everything” is speculation. The student should establish
what is developing, the evidence for it and why it matters to the people being
studied.

Build each discussion from four elements
Development. Identify a specific emerging practice, documented technical development, trial,
adoption pattern or other relevant change.

Evidence. Use a credible source that demonstrates the change or supports the forecast.
Distinguish an announcement or aspiration from an implemented result.

Implication. Explain how it could alter the earlier impacts, the balance between stakeholders or
the answer to the inquiry question.

Qualification. Identify conditions, uncertainty, barriers or plausible limits. An evidence-based
forecast is not a certainty.

Illustrative chain of reasoning
If verified research shows that a particular feedback tool is being redesigned to explain its
suggestions more clearly, a student could explore whether that change might support more
reflective revision. The discussion would need evidence of the redesign and reasons for expecting a
different user response. It should also acknowledge that a pilot or product announcement does not
prove widespread classroom benefits.

How to organise the final section
Allow roughly 2-2.5 minutes, as both presenters recommend. Use a clearly labelled section, present
enough evidence to develop the chosen points and close with an insight about how the inquiry’s
issue may evolve. Avoid a catalogue of unrelated new technologies.

  The presenters differ on the terminology
  Part 1 distinguishes emerging trends as changes happening now and future developments as
  what may come next. Part 2 says they are not distinguished in assessment. Use one coherent,
  clearly labelled section covering the developing picture; do not invent a compulsory split or
  fixed year ranges.

Research this section early. If it is left until recording day, it often becomes unsupported prediction or a rushed
repetition of the main conclusion.

Captions and script

practical choice is clear narration without background music.

  CITATIONS & APPENDICES

Make every important claim traceable
A bibliography cannot replace citations where evidence is used.

  Where  What belongs there

  Section A  Cite the real-world example and borrowed factual evidence at the point of
  use.

  Section B  Place each full source reference beneath its heading, and identify specific
  borrowed material clearly.

  Presentation  Identify the source when the evidence appears or is discussed. The
  webinars accept visual or audio citation; on-screen citation is recommended
  for clarity.

  Visuals and media  Credit images, charts, clips and other media as well as the factual claims. A
  slide may need separate evidence and image citations.

  Reference list in the IPD  Include all sources and relevant materials used in either component, not just
  the three selected for B.

  Appendices  Include supporting evidence the examiner needs to interpret research, with
  labels that the main work can refer to.

A simple citation workflow
Record the source details when researching. Give every useful finding a source locator. Put the
short citation beside the claim on the storyboard or slide. Check that it matches a complete
reference in the IPD. Use one recognised referencing style consistently.

“According to Source 1” alone is too vague. Identify the author or organisation and date, or use a
clear numbered system tied to the references. Ensure the citation remains readable in the
recording.

What an appendix can usefully contain
Interview: role, date, method, questions and transcript.
Survey: instrument, recruitment or selection details, dates, responses, results and limitations.
Other evidence: relevant original material, AI-use records, storyboard or presentation script
where helpful.

  Support the assessed work; do not relocate it
  Appendices are optional overall, but chosen research needs inspectable evidence. Keep the
  actual source evaluation in B and the analysis and essential methodology in the presentation. A
  long appendix does not make up for a weak main argument.

  STUDENT FINAL REVIEW

A high-mark readiness checklist
Check the evidence in the finished work, not just your intention.

A - Focus
□ My question names a manageable digital system, issue and group.
□ I explain a specific, cited real-world example and its connection.
□ My context, content and concept connections are explained, not just named.

B - Sources
□ I evaluate exactly three sources with complete references.
□ I analyse claims, perspectives, evidence and relevant limitations.
□ I explain the particular usefulness of each source to my inquiry.

C - Analysis and evaluation
□ I explain the digital system before relying on its effects.
□ I analyse impacts on people and communities using cited evidence.
□ I compare findings, weigh limitations and reach reasoned judgements.
□ I answer the inquiry question at the end of the analysis.

D - Emerging trends and future developments
□ This is a clearly labelled section after my conclusion of C.
□ My discussion is researched and linked to the inquiry focus.
□ I explain implications and uncertainty instead of making unsupported predictions.
□ I have allowed enough time to develop the discussion.

E - Communication and submission
□ My visuals and narration explain the same points.
□ Citations and evidence are readable; audio and captions are clear.
□ A + B are within 1,500 words and the complete video is within 10 minutes.
□ The IPD contains the complete reference list and necessary supporting evidence.
□ I have checked AI acknowledgements, anonymity and the current file rules.
□ I have watched the complete exported recording.

  Final question
  Can a viewer follow the path from the inquiry question, through the evidence and evaluation, to
  a justified answer and a researched future-facing insight? If that path is unclear, revise the
  argument before adding more design elements.

Source: synthesis of both webinars. This is a revision aid, not an official marking form.
