# EE_03_Reflection_Guide_and_Model

> EE — What a top reflection must contain (7 elements) + a fictional model 500-word reflective statement (Criterion E, 2027) on Met Police live facial recognition.

A perfect reflection should have these ;

  ●​ Your starting perspective: What you initially believed about the digital issue and why you chose to investigate
  it.
  ●​ Your research decisions: How you refined your question, scope or methods, and why those changes helped.
  ●​ A specific challenge: A difficulty you faced, how you responded and how effective your response was.
  ●​ Your judgement of evidence: How you assessed sources, questioned bias or recognised limitations in the
  available data.
  ●​ Changes in your thinking: How the research changed your understanding of the technology, its stakeholders
  or its social consequences.
  ●​ Your growth as a learner: Which skills you developed, supported by concrete examples, and what you would
  do differently.
  ●​ Transfer of learning: How you have used, or specifically plan to use, what you learned in another subject,
  project or real situation.

MODAL EXAMPLE :

Digital Society research question: To what extent has the Metropolitan Police’s use of live facial recognition in London
between 2020 and 2024 achieved a justified balance between public safety and individual privacy?

This fictional model follows the 2027 Criterion E: Reflection requirements and stays below 500 words. The research
experiences should be replaced with the student’s own.

I chose facial recognition because I initially believed that greater surveillance was acceptable if it helped prevent
crime. My early research focused on whether the technology worked. As I examined different perspectives, I realised
that technical accuracy alone could not answer whether its use was justified. This changed both my research question
and my understanding of what a Digital Society investigation involves.

My original question considered facial recognition across the United Kingdom. It was too broad to investigate closely,
and I was collecting examples without a clear basis for comparing them. Focusing on the Metropolitan Police within a
defined period helped me connect the technology to a specific social context. However, this decision also limited my
conclusions: findings from London could not automatically represent other police forces or uses of facial recognition.

One difficulty was comparing sources that measured success differently. Police publications emphasised operational
outcomes, while civil liberties organisations focused on consent, oversight and the consequences of surveillance.
Initially, I treated these as opposing sides and tried to give them equal space. This produced a balanced-looking
discussion, but it did not help me judge the strength of their claims. I created a source table recording each claim, its
supporting evidence, the organisation’s purpose and what the evidence could actually establish. This helped me
recognise that fairness required scrutiny of each argument rather than an equal number of quotations.

I also had to reconsider my use of arrest figures. I had treated arrests following deployments as evidence that facial
recognition reduced crime. When my supervisor asked me to explain that connection, I could not support it. I revised
the argument to distinguish an operational outcome from a wider public safety benefit. This made my conclusion more
cautious, but it also made it more defensible. I learned to check whether my wording claimed more than the evidence
showed.

My assessment of privacy changed too. I had assumed that privacy concerns mainly affected people who were
incorrectly identified. Considering people scanned without becoming suspects made me examine how I was defining
harm. I began asking whose experiences my chosen measures left out. I still considered public safety relevant, but I
became less willing to judge justification through numerical outcomes alone.

My weakest decision was postponing the definition of “justified balance”. I would establish provisional evaluation
criteria earlier in another investigation and revise them openly as my understanding developed.

I have since used the same distinction between a reported outcome and a claimed benefit when analysing a school
proposal for monitoring software. Before accepting that a system improves safety, I now ask what evidence would
demonstrate that improvement and whose interests the evaluation includes.
